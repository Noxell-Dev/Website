You run a business and you want to be on your customers' phones. Someone tells you that you need an app. Someone else says a website is enough. And in the middle appears a third word you didn't know: PWA. The decision feels huge, but it really comes down to a single question: **how often does your customer come back?**

An app is not a better version of a website. They are different channels, for different moments. Choosing wrong doesn't just cost money: an app nobody installs brings you no customers, and a website that can't do what your regular customers need leaves you halfway there. In this article you'll see what each one is for, what a PWA is, and how to decide in which order to invest.

## What is a website for?

A website is your public presence. Anyone can find it on Google, open it from an Instagram or WhatsApp link, and doesn't have to install anything. It lets people discover you, trust you, and buy from you or contact you for the first time.

A website can also do much more than present your business: it can hold a catalogue, sell products, manage bookings, offer a private area for customers, or connect forms to your management system. All from the browser, without going through the app stores.

Its strengths are clear:

- **People find you without knowing you.** A website shows up on Google; an app doesn't. Nobody installs the app of a business they've never heard of.
- **Zero friction.** A link opens in two seconds. No download, no store account, no permissions, no updates.
- **It updates instantly.** You change a text or a price and everyone sees it right away.

## What is a mobile app for?

An app is your channel with people who already know you. The customer who already bought from you and installs it has you on their home screen, receives your notifications, saves their details, and repeats a purchase in seconds. The app isn't for attracting people; it's for **keeping them and for running the business**.

There are things a website does badly or can't do at all: living on the home screen, sending push notifications reliably, using GPS in the background, or working offline. If your business depends on any of those, the app isn't a luxury: it's the tool.

But an app also demands more: it has to be built for Android and for iOS, published in their stores, and maintained every time the operating systems change. There are also fixed costs, like the Apple developer account (around $99 a year). If the budget only stretches to one thing today, it's usually better to do that one thing well.

## The deciding question: how often does your customer come back?

If you sell something people buy once a year or once in a lifetime —a house, a wedding, a course, a legal service— you need a website that convinces, not an app. Nobody installs an application for something they're going to use once.

If your customers buy every week or every month —food, coffee, pharmacy, hair salon, gym, transport— an app makes sense, because the frequency justifies the install. The customer opens it so often that having it one tap away pays off.

In between there's a third case: businesses with regular customers but no daily use. That's where the PWA comes in.

## What is a PWA, and why does it matter here?

A PWA (progressive web app) is a website that behaves almost like an app: it can be added to the phone's home screen, it opens full-screen, and it can partly work offline. The difference is that it doesn't go through the app stores: it lives at a URL, like any website.

That gives it two decisive advantages:

- **It's found like a website.** A URL goes everywhere: your Instagram bio, a WhatsApp conversation, the QR code on your door, or a Google result. And it ranks in search engines like any site.
- **It installs like an app.** Your regular customer adds it to their home screen and always has it at hand, with no downloads or updates.

For many service businesses, a PWA delivers 90% of the benefit of an app at a fraction of the cost. It isn't everything —push notifications on iPhone and some device features are still native-app territory— but it covers the most common case: new customers finding you, and regulars having you one tap away.

## Website, PWA or app: a quick comparison

| Aspect | Website | PWA | Native app |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Found on Google | Yes | Yes | No |
| Something to install | No | Optional, from the browser | Yes, from the store |
| Push notifications | Limited | Yes, with limits on iOS | Yes, full |
| Works offline | No | Partly | Yes |
| Access to GPS, camera and sensors | Limited | Partial | Full |
| Cost and maintenance | Low | Medium | High |
| Best for | Attracting new customers | Businesses with regular customers | Daily, intensive use |

There is no universal winner. The table shows what each option gives up, so you can decide with your eyes open.

## When a website is enough

For most businesses starting out, a well-made website is all you need in the first stage:

- Your audience isn't going to visit you every day.
- You need to validate whether people actually use what you offer before spending on two builds.
- Your priority is being found: if someone searching for your kind of business in your city doesn't find you, the problem is visibility, and a well-made, well-ranked website solves it.
- You want to launch in weeks and measure with real data before deciding the next step.

A responsive website —one that adapts well to the phone— also covers the occasional customer: they check your menu, your hours or your prices by opening a link, without installing anything.

## When an app is worth it

An app is justified when use is intensive and recurring, and when it depends on phone features the browser can't comfortably reach:

- Your customers use you several times a week and repeat purchases or bookings.
- You need reliable push notifications: order updates, appointment reminders, offers for regulars.
- The service depends on the phone: background geolocation, offline work, or deep access to the camera and sensors.
- You already have a customer base that knows you and will install it without hesitating.

A clear example: a taxi or delivery app doesn't exist as a website, because the work happens from the phone and on the move. A restaurant whose customers order three times a month, on the other hand, doesn't need anyone to install anything.

## The order that works for most businesses

The most efficient path for almost every business starting from scratch is this:

1. **First, a well-made responsive website.** One that loads fast, looks good on the phone, and does one thing exceptionally well: getting you found and contacted.
2. **Then, turn that website into a PWA.** Add the option to install it on the home screen and give your regulars quick access without going through the stores.
3. **Finally, the native app, if the data asks for it.** When you have customers coming back daily and you need features the web can't cover, the app reuses the website's catalogue, content and customer base instead of duplicating them.

There are three situations where it does make sense to build both from the start: when the business operation depends on the phone, when you already have a large community waiting for the app, or when daily use is the product itself.

## Common mistakes when deciding

- **Starting with the app before you have customers.** An app with no users is a cost that never pays for itself: nobody searches for it, nobody installs it, and nobody maintains it for you.
- **Thinking the app replaces the website.** The app doesn't rank on Google. Without a website, new customers have no way to find you.
- **Copying what the competition does without asking why.** The business next door having an app doesn't mean you need one: maybe their customers come back daily and yours don't.
- **Forgetting maintenance.** A website updates in minutes; an app has to be updated with every iOS and Android change, with developer accounts renewed and store listings reviewed.
- **Asking for the install too soon.** If the first message a visitor sees is "download our app", most of them leave. Convince first, then offer the install to those who already trust you.

## Checklist for deciding

Answer these questions in order and the decision makes itself:

1. Will your customers use you every week or every month? If the answer is "rarely", start with the website.
2. Does someone who doesn't know you need to find you on Google? If so, the website is mandatory.
3. Do your regular customers come back often enough to install something? If so, consider the PWA.
4. Do you need reliable push notifications, background GPS or offline work? If so, the native app comes into play.
5. Do you already have customers who know you and will install it without hesitating? If not yet, the app can wait.

## Conclusion

The right question isn't "app or website", it's "how often will my customer come back?". If the answer is "rarely", the website wins on cost, speed and reach. If it's "every day" and you also need the phone working for you, then the app starts to make sense. And in the middle, the PWA covers most businesses with regular customers without the cost of a native app.

Start with the website, measure with real data, and decide the next step with information, not gut feeling. Your first digital product doesn't have to be the most advanced one: it has to be the one your customers actually use.
