Copying data from an email into a spreadsheet. Sending the same payment reminder for the third time. Typing out by hand the same reply you already wrote yesterday, and last week, and the month before that. None of these tasks grows your business, yet together they eat up a huge share of the week.

It is not just your impression: a Ricoh study across several European countries found that employees in Spain spend an average of around 16 hours a week on administrative tasks and manual processes — nearly two working days. Research from Smartsheet points the same way: more than 40% of workers say repetitive tasks take up at least a quarter of their working week.

The good news is that much of that time can be won back. You do not need to transform the whole business at once, and you do not need to know how to code: you need to find where the time is going and tackle the right tasks first.

## First, measure: a one-week audit

Most businesses cannot say exactly where their hours go, because repetitive tasks hide inside the routine: they take five or ten minutes and look harmless. The problem is the total they add up to.

### The one-week log

For one week, write down (you, and everyone on your team) every manual task that repeats: what it is, how many times it happens, and how long it takes each time. A shared spreadsheet with three columns is enough. At the end of the week, do the maths: a 10-minute task repeated 6 times a day is 5 hours a week. That is usually where the surprises show up.

### The four-question rule

Not everything repetitive deserves to be automated. To decide, run each task through these four questions:

1. **Does it happen often?** At least once a week.
2. **Does it always follow the same steps?** If it is done the same way every time, with no complex decisions in between, it is a candidate.
3. **Does it move a lot of data or repeat many times?** The higher the volume, the bigger the saving.
4. **Does it produce human errors?** Mistyped figures, forgotten emails, duplicated data: the classic sign.

If a task answers "yes" to three of the four, it deserves attention. If it answers yes to all four, you probably should have dealt with it a long time ago.

## The repetitive tasks almost every business has

Every business is different, but the time thieves tend to be the same ones:

### Copying and pasting data between apps

The order that arrives by email and gets typed into a spreadsheet; the new customer who gets entered manually in two or three different places; the website form whose data someone transcribes afterwards. Every manual copy is lost time and a chance to get a number or a letter wrong.

### Invoices, quotes and payment reminders

Issuing the same kind of invoice over and over, renaming files, sending the quote as a PDF and then, days later, chasing payment with reminders written one by one. This is predictable work from start to finish, and therefore among the first things that can be systematised with templates and automatic reminders.

### Answering the same questions again and again

Opening hours, rough prices, how to get there, turnaround times, what a service includes. If your inbox and your WhatsApp are full of the same five questions, the answer is not typing faster: it is having ready-made replies, a genuinely useful FAQ section and, when volume justifies it, automated answers for the basics — leaving people the conversations that actually require judgement.

### Appointments, bookings and confirmations

The back-and-forth of messages to arrange a single appointment ("does Tuesday work for you?", "not Tuesday, how about Thursday?") takes far more time than it seems. A booking link where the customer picks a slot, with automatic confirmation and reminder, removes that whole chain of messages and reduces no-shows too.

### Reports assembled by hand

The weekly or monthly report that someone builds by copying figures from three different places into a table. If the data already exists in your tools, opening the report should be all it takes — not rebuilding it from scratch every time.

## How to eliminate them, step by step

### 1. Fix the process before you automate it

Automating a messy process only produces mess faster. If everyone does the task a different way, first agree on a single way of doing it — simple and written down. Often, just standardising (a template, a checklist, one single place where the data lives) already removes much of the time and most of the errors, before you touch any tool.

### 2. Start with the highest-impact task, not the easiest one

The temptation is to start with whatever is simplest, but your audit numbers should lead: hours the task costs per month multiplied by how many people suffer it. A task that steals 30 minutes a day from three people adds up to roughly 30 hours a month; that is your first candidate, even if it takes a bit more work to set up than another one that only saves one person five minutes.

### 3. Use what you already have first

Before paying for anything new, look at the tools you already use: invoicing software usually supports recurring invoices and automatic payment reminders; email supports templates and scheduled replies; a spreadsheet can update itself from a form. And if you need to connect apps to each other, there are no-code automation platforms (the "when this happens, do that" kind) with free tiers or plans costing a few euros a month to get started, designed for people who do not code.

### 4. Automate one single task and measure the result

The most common mistake is trying to automate everything in the same week and giving up on all of it the week after. Pick one task, get the solution running, check over a few weeks that it works, and note how much time it actually frees up. That first success, measured in real hours, is what convinces the team (and you) to move on to the next one.

### 5. Leave to people the work that needs judgement

Not everything can — or should — be automated. A negotiation, a sensitive complaint, a decision that depends on context: there, the value is precisely the person. Well-planned automation does not replace that work; it makes it possible, by freeing up the hours that today go into copying, pasting and remembering. It is also worth checking from time to time that what you automated still works: a short monthly review stops a broken workflow from going unnoticed.

## In short: your plan for this week

1. Log every repetitive task and the time it takes for one week.
2. Run each one through the four questions: frequency, fixed steps, volume and errors.
3. Sort the list by hours lost per month, not by how easy each fix looks.
4. Standardise the first task before automating it.
5. Solve it with what you already have or with a simple tool, and measure the time you get back.

Repetitive tasks are not eliminated by wishing them away or by working faster: they are eliminated by spotting them, measuring them and tackling them one at a time. Start with the one stealing the most hours from you. With just that one, next week will already look different from this one.
