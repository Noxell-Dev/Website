Every morning you do more or less the same thing: you check your email from your phone, open a shared document, watch a show, order something from an app, and maybe message a colleague in a work tool. You did not install any of those programs from a disc, and none of them were sold to you "forever". They all work the same way: you open the browser or the app, sign in, and that's it. That, even if we don't call it that, is SaaS.

SaaS stands for Software as a Service. And understanding what it means explains why software has changed so much in recent years: we stopped buying programs and started subscribing to them.

## What Is SaaS?

A SaaS product is an application that is not installed on your computer or bought once, but lives on the provider's servers and is used over the internet, usually for a monthly or yearly subscription.

The difference from traditional software is simple. In the past you bought a license, installed the program on your machine, and took care of everything: updating it, making backups, and reinstalling it if you changed computers. With SaaS, the provider handles all of that. You just sign in with your account from any device and always use the latest version.

It's the same difference as buying a film on DVD versus watching it on a streaming platform: in the first case the product is yours, but you have to store and maintain it; in the second, someone else takes care of everything and you pay to access it whenever you want.

## Examples of SaaS You Use Without Realizing It

You probably use several SaaS products every day without thinking about it:

- **Email and office tools:** Gmail and Google Workspace, Microsoft 365.
- **Storage and files:** Google Drive, Dropbox.
- **Communication and teamwork:** Slack, Zoom, Notion.
- **Entertainment:** Netflix, Spotify, YouTube.
- **Business management:** invoicing software, CRMs like HubSpot or Salesforce, booking or email marketing tools.

Some are free for personal use and paid in their professional versions; others are paid from day one. But the model is the same: the software runs in the cloud and you access it over the internet.

## Why Almost Everything Works This Way Now

SaaS took over thanks to a combination of advantages for the user and for whoever builds the software.

### For the people who use it

- **You start in minutes.** No long installation or setup: you create an account and you're already working.
- **It works on any device.** You start a document on your computer and continue it on your phone, because your data lives in the cloud, not on one specific machine.
- **Always up to date.** Improvements and security fixes arrive on their own, without you having to download anything.
- **Low upfront cost.** Instead of paying a large license fee all at once, you pay a small monthly fee, and you can cancel whenever you want.
- **It grows with you.** If your team grows from 2 to 20 people, you add users without buying servers or reinstalling anything.

### For the people who build it

The provider earns recurring, predictable revenue instead of one-off sales, can improve the product continuously, and can reach users all over the world without shipping boxes or discs. That's why so many programs that used to be sold as a one-time purchase have become subscriptions.

## SaaS, Cloud, IaaS and PaaS: Not All the Same

It's easy to confuse these terms because they all relate to the cloud, but they don't mean the same thing:

- **SaaS (software as a service):** the complete application, ready to use. Example: an online invoicing tool.
- **PaaS (platform as a service):** an environment for developers to build and publish their own applications without managing servers.
- **IaaS (infrastructure as a service):** servers, storage and networks rented by the hour, like those from Amazon Web Services or Microsoft Azure.

Put simply: with IaaS you rent the land, with PaaS you rent the workshop, and with SaaS you rent the finished product.

## The Drawbacks Worth Knowing

The model isn't perfect, and it's worth knowing its weak points before relying on a tool:

- **You depend on the internet.** Without a connection, there's no access. In areas with poor coverage or during a network outage, work stops.
- **You don't control the software.** If the provider raises the price, redesigns the tool or shuts down the service, it affects you directly, even if you can export your data.
- **Your data sits on someone else's servers.** That's why it matters to check what security and privacy guarantees the provider offers, and where your information is stored.
- **Subscriptions add up.** One subscription looks cheap, but five or ten tools at once can add up to a considerable monthly bill. It's worth reviewing from time to time what you actually still use.

None of these drawbacks makes the model bad, but they do explain why not everything should be SaaS: there are cases, such as highly sensitive systems or places without a stable connection, where locally installed software still makes sense.

## How to Choose a SaaS Product Well

If you're thinking about using a tool like this for your business or your daily life, these questions will help you choose well:

1. **Can I export my data?** Avoid tools that lock you in: check that you can take your information out in a standard format.
2. **What does each plan include?** Compare users, storage and features, not just the price of the cheapest plan.
3. **What happens if I cancel?** Read the terms: how long they keep your data and whether you can recover it afterwards.
4. **What security does it offer?** Backups, encryption and two-factor authentication are reasonable minimums.
5. **Will I actually use it?** Try the free version or the trial period before committing to a yearly plan.

## Conclusion

At its core, SaaS is software you use instead of software you buy: it lives in the cloud, it's paid for by subscription, and it maintains itself. That's why almost everything you use every day — from email to streaming to work tools — works this way: it's more convenient to start, easier to maintain, and more flexible than the old buy-and-install model.

The next time you pay a monthly subscription and wonder why the program is no longer sold "once and for all", that's the answer: you're not buying a product, you're hiring a service. And knowing how it works, you can choose better which ones deserve your money and which ones don't.
