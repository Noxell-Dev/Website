You launched your online store full of hope: you picked the products, uploaded the photos, set up payments... and weeks go by without a single order. Or worse: your stats show visitors, people browse products, but nobody buys.

It's one of the most frustrating situations in ecommerce, but it's almost never bad luck. Behind a store that doesn't sell there's usually one (or several) of these causes, and almost all of them can be fixed without rebuilding the store from scratch.

First, two numbers for perspective: on average, only about 1.4% of visits to an online store end in a purchase (Statista, 2026), and 70.22% of started carts are abandoned before payment (Baymard Institute). In other words, even healthy stores "lose" most of their visitors. That's not the problem; the problem is when your store loses more than normal, and that almost always has a concrete reason.

## 1. Hardly anyone visits your store (or the wrong people do)

It sounds obvious, but it's the first check: no visits, no sales. And not just any visit counts: if your traffic comes from curious browsers with no intent to buy, conversion will be zero no matter what you do.

**How to fix it:** open your store's stats (or Google Analytics) and look at where traffic comes from and what visitors do. If there are barely any visits, the problem is upstream of the store: Google rankings, social media presence, a Google Shopping listing, or ad campaigns. If there are visits but they leave within seconds, the problem is inside: keep reading.

## 2. Your product pages don't convince anyone

Most stores that don't sell fail here: small, dark photos copied from the supplier's catalog; two-line descriptions copied from the manufacturer; no measurements, no materials, nothing explaining who the product is for or what problem it solves.

Online shoppers can't touch the product. The product page has to do all the work: make them picture themselves using it.

**How to fix it:** your own photos, large and from several angles (with the product in use when possible); descriptions that talk about benefits, not just features; a size or measurement chart where relevant; visible stock ("only 3 left"); and a clear price from the start, no fine print.

## 3. Nobody trusts your store

Buying from an unknown store feels risky, and rightly so. If your site doesn't project trust, visitors will go buy the same thing from a big marketplace even if it costs more. The signals shoppers look for: reviews from other customers, a clear returns policy, real contact details (not just a form), a visible legal notice, and a secure connection.

**How to fix it:** ask every customer for a review and show them on the product pages; publish a simple, easy-to-find returns policy; display a phone number or email address prominently and answer quickly; make sure the site uses HTTPS and shows secure-payment badges at checkout. Trust isn't claimed: it's proven in the details.

## 4. The final price scares customers away

This is the number-one cause of abandoned carts worldwide: according to the Baymard Institute, extra costs (shipping, taxes, surcharges) revealed at the end of the process cause up to 48% of abandonments. The customer decides to buy with a price in mind, and if at the last step they discover it's €8 more for shipping, they feel tricked and leave.

**How to fix it:** show shipping costs from the product page (a postcode calculator works); if you can, offer free shipping above a threshold and say so prominently ("free shipping over €50"); break down taxes from the start, never at the last step. The final price should never be a surprise.

## 5. Buying from your store is a chore

Every unnecessary step in the purchase flow is an exit door. The usual culprits: forcing account creation before paying (18–26% of shoppers abandon because of this), a four- or five-screen checkout, endless forms on mobile, and few payment methods. If you don't offer the method the customer wants to use (card, PayPal, digital wallets...), many won't even try another one: they leave.

**How to fix it:** allow guest checkout with no mandatory registration; cut the checkout down to the minimum steps and fields; offer the most-used payment methods in your country alongside cards and PayPal; and run through the whole process yourself on your phone before blaming the market.

## 6. Your store isn't designed for mobile

Over 70% of most stores' traffic comes from mobile, and mobile cart abandonment runs close to 80%. Many stores are designed and tested on the owner's computer, and on a phone they're a different story: tiny buttons, text you have to pinch-zoom, forms that are impossible to fill in.

**How to fix it:** run your next full test purchase from your own phone, no cheating. If it's hard for you, imagine a customer in a hurry. Big buttons, readable type, fast-loading images, and a checkout you can complete one-handed: that's the minimum today.

## 7. The store loads slowly and people leave before seeing anything

Every extra second of loading costs sales: impatient visitors don't wait, and Google also ranks slow sites lower. Uncompressed images, too many plugins, or cheap hosting are usually to blame.

**How to fix it:** measure your speed with PageSpeed Insights and tackle the basics: compress your images, remove plugins and scripts you don't use, and consider better hosting if the server is slow to respond. Speed is one of the few factors that can lift sales within days.

## How to diagnose it in 10 minutes

If you don't know where to start, follow this order:

1. Check your stats: are there visits? Where do they come from? On which page do they leave?
2. Make a full test purchase from your phone, as if you were a customer who doesn't know you.
3. Note every point of friction you find: prices that change, extra steps, confusing copy, anything that doesn't inspire trust.
4. Ask two or three people who don't know the store to try buying something and think aloud at each step.
5. Fix what shows up on that list first: it's usually exactly what's blocking sales.

In most cases the problem sits in the first three steps of this list and gets resolved in days, not months.

## Conclusion

An online store that doesn't sell is almost never bad luck: it's lack of visits, unconvincing product pages, distrust, price surprises, or a purchase flow with too much friction. Go through the seven causes in this guide in order and tackle the first one you find. The stores that sell aren't the ones with the most traffic, but the ones that convert best the traffic they already have... and that can be improved starting today.
