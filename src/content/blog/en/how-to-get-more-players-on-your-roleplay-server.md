The server is built, the jobs work, the economy is tuned… and at peak time there are four people online, two of them staff. It is the most common situation for roleplay servers, especially in FiveM and GTA RP: the technical work is done, but the players never arrive — and the few who do join don't come back the next day.

The temptation is to promote harder: post in more places, more often, more loudly. But promoting a server that doesn't retain anyone is like pouring water into a leaky bucket. This guide follows the order that actually works: first fix why people leave, then make yourself findable, and finally give players reasons to stay and bring others with them.

## The starting mistake: driving players to a server that doesn't retain them

A new player decides whether your server deserves a second session within minutes, and that decision outweighs any advert. If they log in, can't figure out what to do and find an empty city, they won't return — even if your social posts cross their path ten more times.

### The vicious circle of empty servers

Roleplay is a social activity: almost nobody stays where there is nobody to roleplay with. With few concurrent players, interactions dry up, the people online get bored and leave, and the server looks even emptier to the next person who joins. Breaking that circle is the priority; everything else is secondary until you have a minimum base of players online at the same hours.

### Retention is cheaper than acquisition

Winning a new player costs time or money: content, ads, hours spent in communities. Winning a second day from someone who already joined costs far less: making their first session go well. So before any campaign, spend a week looking at your server through a newcomer's eyes.

## Sum up in one sentence why anyone would choose your server

Open any server list and read twenty descriptions in a row: nearly all of them promise the same things. "Realistic economy, custom jobs, active staff, friendly community." If your pitch can be copied into another server without changing a comma, a player who is comparing has no reason to pick you.

### Find your real difference

You don't need to invent the impossible: one concrete, verifiable difference is enough. Some examples that genuinely work as a hook:

- **A clear roleplay style:** serious, semi-serious or laid-back — and rules that consistently back it up.
- **A defined language and schedule:** a Spanish-speaking community that concentrates its activity in the evenings has a real edge over international servers that are empty at your hours.
- **Content that isn't everywhere:** a well-built faction, a jobs system with real progression, story events with continuity.
- **Honest size:** a small server where everyone knows everyone can be the pitch, not a flaw to hide.

### Put it where the decision is made

That sentence belongs in the first line of your server listing, in the name or short description if it fits, and at the top of your Discord and your website. A player takes seconds to decide whether to keep reading; if what makes you different shows up in the third paragraph, it doesn't show up at all.

## Reduce the friction of joining

Every step between "I'm interested in this server" and "I'm actually roleplaying" loses people. Walk through your own process as a stranger and count the steps: how many forms, channels and waits stand between a visitor and playing?

### What scares newcomers off most

- **Endless whitelists:** an application nobody reviews within 48 hours is a lost player. If you use an access list, set response times and keep them — or open public access slots while you grow.
- **Impossible rulebooks:** eighty pages of rules before joining guarantee that nobody reads them. Summarise the essentials on one page and link the rest.
- **A labyrinthine Discord:** if getting three roles and reading six channels is what it takes to find how to connect, there are too many steps.
- **Downloads and errors with no guide:** a short install guide, with the most common errors and their fixes, stops you losing players silently in front of an error message.

The point isn't to remove the filters — a serious server needs them — but to make every step fast, clear and owned by someone who actually replies.

## Look after the first session: that's where you win or lose the player

If a new player spawns in an empty spot, with no money, no clear job and nobody crossing their path for ten minutes, their conclusion will be "there's nothing to do here" — even if your server is packed with content in other areas.

### What should happen in the first few minutes

- **Spawning where life is:** the arrival point should sit in an area with regular activity, not in a pretty but deserted corner of the map.
- **Having something to do right away:** a first guided task — sorting their documents, a simple first job, being shown a key business — gives them a goal while they discover the city.
- **Meeting real people:** let staff and veteran players know when someone new joins. A greeting and a first roleplay scene are worth more than any welcome pack.
- **Knowing they can ask:** a questions channel that gets answered within minutes, and a friendly tone, lower the embarrassment every beginner feels.

A useful trick: create a fresh account with no privileges and join as a stranger, once at peak time and once off-peak. What you feel in that session is, almost always, exactly what your new players feel.

## Make yourself findable: listings, Discord and your own website

Once the server retains people, it's time to be visible. You don't need to be everywhere: you need to be good in the places where roleplay players actually look for a server.

### Optimise your page on server listings

Your listing is your main shop window. A name carrying language and roleplay style, a description that leads with your difference, real screenshots of your city — not generic captures — and details updated whenever anything changes. Check that the Discord link and connection details work: more than one listing loses players to an expired invite.

### Discord: a community that looks alive, not a formality

For almost every player, your Discord is the pre-visit before joining. If the last announcement is a month old, they'll assume the server is dead — even if it isn't. Keep visible signs of life going without having to post daily: event announcements, photos from scenes, changelogs, and public channels where conversation is visible before you ask for sign-up or verification.

### A simple website that answers questions

A simple page covering how to join, what kind of roleplay happens, the active factions and businesses, and the frequent questions will capture searches from people who are already comparing servers. It doesn't need to be complex: it needs to load fast, tell the truth about the server, and reach the Discord or the joining guide in one click.

## Show the roleplay with short content — don't describe it

Players don't trust the adjectives in your description; they trust what they see. Short clips — a well-roleplayed chase, a funny moment in a business, an event with a full city — are currently the most effective way to showcase a roleplay server, because they show exactly the experience a newcomer will get.

### What to post, and how often

- **Clips from real scenes**, with the permission of the people in them, over produced trailers: the genuine article converts better.
- **Recognisable series:** the day-to-day of one faction, beats from an ongoing storyline, what's new in each update.
- **A sustainable rhythm:** two or three pieces a week that you can keep up beat ten in one week followed by silence.
- **Small creators before big ones:** a modest streamer whose community fits your style of roleplay can bring players who stay; a huge one may only bring a one-day visit. Invite them to actually roleplay, not to "drop by and say hi".

Ask your own players to share their clips and moments: community content sounds like the truth — and people love seeing themselves in it.

## Events and updates: a calendar that builds a habit

Players return when they know something is happening. A fixed weekly event — races, a market, a storyline that moves forward every Friday — turns your server into an appointment, and appointments fill peak hours, which is exactly what breaks the vicious circle from the start of this article.

Announce every event in advance on Discord and in-game, with a clear time, and afterwards share how it went, with photos or clips: that recap is recognition for those who came and advertising for those who didn't. The same goes for updates: a steady flow of small, visible improvements says "this server is alive" better than one huge update every six months.

## Your current players are your best channel

Roleplay is played in groups: people arrive with their faction, their roleplay partner or their two usual friends — and they leave when their group leaves. That makes every happy player a complete recruitment channel.

- **Make inviting easy:** clear invite links and, if you like, a small, non-competitive reward for bringing friends who stay — never advantages that break the balance.
- **Welcome whole groups:** if a faction is looking for a home, give them a planned landing: talk to them, explain the open slots and storylines, and help them through their first day. A group that arrives together stays together.
- **Look after your veterans:** recognising the people who sustain scenes, businesses and factions — with a real say in decisions, not just a rank — is what maintains the base that newcomers join into.

## Measure what works and fix what doesn't

Without data, you'll change things at random every week. With four simple numbers, you'll know where your problem is:

- **New joins per week:** if there are very few, your problem is visibility.
- **Players who return on day two and day seven:** if people join but don't return, your problem is the first session or the activity in your off-peak hours.
- **Peak concurrent players, and when it happens:** always measure against your peak; concentrate events and staff in that window.
- **Where each player came from:** use different invite links for each channel — listing, socials, a streamer — and you'll know which effort deserves more of your time.

### A four-week plan to get started

- **Week 1:** join as a stranger, note every friction point, and fix joining and the first session.
- **Week 2:** rewrite your one-sentence difference and apply it to the listing, the Discord and the website.
- **Week 3:** start the short-content rhythm with real clips from the server.
- **Week 4:** launch a fixed weekly event and the invitation system, and review your four numbers.

## Conclusion

Getting more players on a roleplay server isn't a promotion trick: it's a chain. A clear pitch makes people choose you, a frictionless join makes them arrive, a good first session makes them stay, events and content make them return, and the players who return bring the next ones. Audit your chain at its weakest link — it's almost always the first session, not the advertising — and growth stops depending on luck.
